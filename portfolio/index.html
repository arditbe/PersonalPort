I never got a computer science degree. I pre-trained myself.

School doesn't teach you. It fine-tunes what you already know.

I went to a computer science high school, and that is the whole of my formal education in this. Everything else came from documentation, from source code, and from breaking things until they made sense. A degree for what is already on the internet, free, is unnecessary — and I would rather spend those four years shipping.

Fine-Tuning Makes Specialists: Why Fine-Tuned Models Behave Like Narrow AI

Grow Labs · 22 September 2026

Auditor: An Autonomous Agent for Continuous Evaluation of Fine-Tuned Models

22 September 2026 · the paper behind the repository

Anyone can fine-tune a model on a laptop now, but almost nobody can tell whether the result is any good. Auditor writes fresh probes for every run, has a separate judge commit to its criteria before it sees the answer, makes one probe in four a deliberate trap for hallucination, and drills harder wherever a score comes back weak. It exists because I fine-tuned Mistral-7B on Romani-language books and had no honest way to know if I had improved it.

Do AI Benchmarks Matter?

Grow Labs · 22 September 2026

A benchmark score is one number, and I argue it is worth far less than the headlines it sells. Goodhart’s law applies the moment labs compete on a table. The test setup alone can move a result by six points — Fable 5.1 was reported at both 67.4% and 73.7% on the same benchmark. Add contamination, saturation, and the fact that companies choose which scores to show, and a two-point lead means almost nothing.

I was ten when I started learning how computers actually work. It is still the best thing I have ever done.

It started with BAT files and a stubborn attempt to break into my own copy of Windows. Ten years later I train language models from scratch and I am starting my own AI lab. Nobody ever taught me any of it — no course, no bootcamp, no degree. It is the same curiosity. The machines just got bigger.

Why AppleSteve Jobs, and what he taught me

I love Apple, and Steve Jobs is the reason I build anything at all. He is the one who taught me that the way a thing feels to use is not decoration on top of the engineering — it is the engineering.

That is why I care so much about models running on the devices people already have. A model nobody can run is not a finished piece of work.

Age 10: BAT files, and the antivirus for my own virusesWhere software actually started

I started with simple BAT files, trying to crack my own copy of Windows just to see whether I could. Then I wrote viruses — several of them — for no reason other than wanting to know how they worked.

Then I wrote the antivirus for them. That turn is the part I still think about: the interesting problem was never breaking the machine, it was understanding it well enough to defend it from what I had just built. Everything since has been a version of that.

14 and 15: Python, then five languagesEntirely self-taught

At fourteen I started learning Python on my own. I have never taken a course in technology — not one, at any point. Everything came from documentation, source code and breaking things until they made sense.

By fifteen I was working in five programming languages. That is also when it stopped being about languages: the interesting part was no longer the syntax but what you could make several systems do together.

2020: TubifyMy first platform

I built a social media platform called Tubify. It is where everything I had taught myself stopped being separate tricks and became one system — accounts, feeds, media, storage, all of it having to agree with each other at the same time.

Learning to connect things is a different skill from learning to build them, and Tubify is where I learned it.

2022: Amaro Than PlatformsWritten line by line

I started Amaro Than Platforms and coded it myself, line by line — every service, every endpoint, the whole backend. No team writing it around me, no framework doing the thinking.

That is where my software engineering grew up, and it is why I can say exactly why every part of it behaves the way it does.

2022: my first model20M parameters, 10,000 samples

20 million parameters, trained from scratch on my own laptop, on 10,000 samples. I chose that size because it was what one machine could hold, and it turned out to be the right place to start. A model that small is cheap enough to train again when you are wrong, and small enough that you can see why you were wrong.

The plot further down this page is that model: three runs, the overfitting gap falling from above 2.9 to 0.1851. Everything I know about learning rate, dropout and temperature came from breaking it first.

Narrow AI, then scaleANI, then 12B and 32B

I built ANI — artificial narrow intelligence, systems that are excellent at exactly one domain. That is where the small models went. It is also why the question in my second paper is not abstract for me: a narrow model is superb until you show it something from outside its domain, and then it has nothing. I have watched exactly where they stop.

The work now runs at 12 billion and 32 billion parameters on NVIDIA GPUs in Google Cloud. Nothing about the method changed. Only the cost of being wrong did.

What I am doing nextLeaving Amaro Than for Grow Labs

I am leaving Amaro Than Platforms soon. It is where my software and backend work started, and I am proud of it, but my attention belongs in one place now.

That place is Grow Labs — my own AI lab. Full focus, on the one problem I actually care about: models that keep learning after they are deployed, instead of freezing the moment training ends.

What I do

Four stages. Open any one to read how it actually goes.

Pre-training20M to 32B parameters

Building the model from nothing: tokenizer, embeddings, transformer layers, output head, then the training loop that grinds cross-entropy loss down. I have run this from 20 million parameters on a laptop up to 32 billion on NVIDIA GPUs in Google Cloud — the same six stages at both ends, with very different consequences for getting them wrong.

Post-trainingWhere a model becomes usable

The stage that decides whether a model is usable or useless. Learning rate, dropout, weight decay and sampling temperature are what separate coherent text from a loop or a confident lie.

Getting these four right was the biggest lesson of my work so far — and I learned it by getting all four wrong first.

Data developmentThe stage everything else inherits

Collecting, cleaning and structuring the corpus as JSONL, one training example per line, with a validation set held back so I can tell learning apart from memorising.

If a fact is not in the data, the model cannot know it. Everything downstream inherits this stage's mistakes.

Running it on bad hardwarePhones, low-end PCs, plain CPUs

Models and apps built for phones, low-end PCs and CPU-only inference. Most of the world does not have an H100.

I would rather ship something that runs on what people already own than something that needs a datacentre to say hello.

From the training log

The numbers behind the first paper, and the mistakes behind the numbers.

How the overfitting gap came downThree runs, 2022

Validation loss minus training loss. Under 1.0 is the line I treat as real generalisation. Doubling the dataset cut the gap by about 68% — more data fixed overfitting harder than any other change I tried.

The four numbers that decided everythingWhat broke, and what fixed it
What the model didCauseFix
Training went unstable, output loopedLearning rate 0.050.0001
Made things up, confidentlyDropout and weight decay too low0.15 / 0.02
Repeated the same wordsTemperature 0.10.25
Went random, invented factsTemperature 0.80.25

Temperature is the one worth explaining. Low temperature always picks the most likely next token, so the model repeats. High temperature reaches for unlikely ones, so it talks nonsense. For my model, 0.25 was the balance.

Auditor

Python · Google ADK · Apache-2.0

An agent that audits your fine-tuned model on its own, on a schedule. It writes its own probes, has a separate judge score every answer against criteria fixed before it sees the response, then drills deeper into whatever scored badly. Every failure becomes a training example with a corrected answer. Built for a hackathon on Vertex AI, Firestore and Cloud Run.

ReShoot

Python · JavaScript · local Ollama

A local-first metadata auditor and editor for photographs. Describe a shot in plain English and it rewrites the file’s EXIF, XMP and IPTC tags to match, then tells you how convincing the result actually is. Golden hour is computed from the NOAA solar equations rather than guessed, exposure is solved against the lens’s real aperture range, and your original file is never touched.
